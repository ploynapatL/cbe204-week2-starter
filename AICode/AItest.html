A campus you can read
like a trail map.

Kestrel Ridge is built up a forested slope, not around a quad — so your first year of chemistry sits eight hundred feet from the watershed you'll spend your last year studying. 2,400 students, four schools, one ridge.

The ridge, mapped

Your major decides your elevation.

Kestrel Ridge climbs 820 vertical feet from the valley floor to the field station. Rather than scatter departments across a flat quad, we built the campus so that where you study reflects what you study — foundational classrooms low on the slope, specialized labs and studios higher up, and fieldwork at the summit.

LOWER CAMPUS · 420–560 FT

Foundations & Letters

First-year seminars, the library, and the School of Letters & Culture sit closest to the valley — and to Alder Hollow itself, ten minutes downhill on foot.

MID CAMPUS · 700–880 FT

Labs & Studios

Engineering, materials, and design work happens in the terraced lab buildings, wired for equipment too heavy to carry uphill twice.

UPPER CAMPUS · 1,050–1,240 FT

Field & Observation

The Alder Creek Field Station and the ridge observatory sit at the tree line, where the coursework is the landscape itself.

Four schools

Academics, by the slope.

Every school keeps its classes small enough that faculty learn your name in week one — a 9:1 student-to-faculty ratio, university-wide.

500 FT · Lower Campus

School of Environmental Science

Watershed ecology, soil science, and climate modeling, taught from a stream that runs directly through the department's back door.

560 FT · Lower Campus

School of Letters & Culture

Writing, history, and languages, anchored by a small-press program that binds and prints its own student journal each spring.

820 FT · Mid Campus

School of Engineering & Design

Mechanical and environmental engineering alongside product design, sharing a single fabrication floor so majors keep colliding.

1,140 FT · Upper Campus

School of Field Biology

Wildlife biology and conservation, based out of the field station, with coursework that runs on the seasons more than the semester.

Alder Creek Field Station

The lab with no walls on one side.

Every school sends students through the field station, not just biology. Engineers instrument the creek for flow data. Writers keep a season log. Environmental science majors practically live there. It's the one building on campus every graduate has a key story about.
